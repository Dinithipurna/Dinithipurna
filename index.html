Wearables that sense the person, not just the scene.

My vision

Wearables that adapt to the person wearing them. Most assistants react to the scene: what the camera sees and the microphone hears. I want them to also read the wearer, their attention, load and state, and change when and how they help.

HeadRoom

A lightweight, edge-deployable pipeline for adaptive notification routing

HeadRoom predicts the next moment of egocentric video and audio. When a stream is hard to predict, that sense is probably busy. It routes each notification to the channel with more room. In a controlled study with 25 participants, routing to the more available channel reduced response time under high perceptual load compared with routing to the less available one.

  • Runs in real time on wearable and mobile edge hardware
  • Distinguished Poster Award, Meta’s Research Summit for Egocentric Intelligence 2026

Sensory Spotlight

Anticipating shifts in attention for proactive assistance

Proactive assistance depends on timing. Support that arrives after attention has moved is easily missed, and running every sensor continuously is costly for a device worn all day. Sensory Spotlight investigates how lightweight wearable signals, such as eye and head movement, can anticipate when a wearer’s attention is about to shift and where it will land, so that assistance, and the more demanding sensors behind it, are engaged only when they are needed.

A systematic review of 63 papers

How do adaptive wearables sense user state, decide when to act, and choose how to intervene? We propose a taxonomy of sensing modalities, adaptation triggers and intervention strategies, and set out the open design challenges for the next generation of assistive wearables.

Looks Fine to Me

Understanding oversight in agentic interactions

As AI agents take over execution, the human job becomes oversight: noticing when an output needs a closer look. In a within-subject study with 36 participants, people completed Python tasks with a coding agent that returned correct code, a silent defect or a visible failure, while we recorded egocentric video, gaze, physiological signals and interaction logs.

Participants caught visible failures readily but often missed silent defects while feeling highly confident. They ran generic checks, did not gather diagnostic evidence, or misread the evidence they had. Feeling vigilant and being vigilant are not the same thing.

VRSense

An explainable system to help mitigate cybersickness in VR games

A plug-and-play tool that helps VR developers assess cybersickness. It uses interpretable features instead of a black box, so developers see which design choices and interactions are making players sick.
